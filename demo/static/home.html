Horizon scanning for the crypto AML rulebook

Fraud moves in weeks. Rulebooks move in years.
Drift Sentinel measures the gap.

Drift Sentinel is an agentic system that continuously tests a crypto AML/CFT rulebook against the fraud that exists today — both the typologies already documented and ones the system invents itself. Every gap it finds is independently re-tested, drafted as a candidate red flag, and put in front of a named analyst who accepts, amends or rejects it. Nothing reaches guidance on its own.

Why this exists
A rulebook written before the fraud existed

The red-flag indicators that still anchor crypto detection were published by the FATF in September 2020, in Virtual Assets – Red Flag Indicators of Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing. They were drawn from case studies collected between 2017 and 2020 — before generative AI put real-time deepfake liveness bypass, cloned voices, synthetic identity documents and replica exchange apps within reach of an ordinary fraudster.

South Africa layered its travel-rule obligations on top of that base with FIC Directive 9, gazetted 15 November 2024 and in force from 30 April 2025. Directive 9 tells a crypto asset service provider what information must travel with a transfer. It does not tell anyone what an AI-assisted attack on that control looks like.

The lag is the bottleneck

Updating guidance is slow and manual. A scam appears, victims lose money, authorities investigate, the FATF or INTERPOL publish, regulators update expectations, and only then do controls change. Typologies mutate in weeks; the rulebook updates in years.

The stakes are concrete. South Africa left the FATF grey list on 24 October 2025 after 32 months under increased monitoring, and the next mutual evaluation is already on the horizon. Detection that quietly falls behind the fraud is how that ground gets lost.

Drift Sentinel sits in the market-monitoring and horizon-scanning stage of the regulatory life cycle, and hands into the policy stage.

Who it is for, and why
Financial Intelligence Centre Typologies analyst

The FIC receives suspicious transaction reports, issues directives to accountable institutions, and publishes the red-flag guidance that CASPs are measured against. It owns the artefact this system tests: the indicator set itself.

The end-user is a typologies analyst. Instead of reading hundreds of pages of threat reporting each quarter, they open a short queue of verified, cited gap candidates, each mapped to an attack technique and paired with a drafted indicator.

Financial Sector Conduct Authority Supervision specialist

The FSCA licenses and supervises crypto asset service providers as financial services providers, so it carries the conduct and market-integrity consequences when a control stops catching what it was written to catch.

The end-user is a supervision specialist. A gap report tells them which obligations an AI-assisted attack walks past, before it shows up in a supervisory finding or a consumer-loss statistic.

How a check works
  1. Retrieval grounds the run in the current rulebook and the documented typology corpus, so every finding can be traced back to a source document.
  2. Reconciliation instantiates each documented typology as a synthetic scenario and steps it through the rulebook rule by rule, recording which indicators fire and which are evaded.
  3. Generation reasons from a library of AI capability primitives to invent evasion paths no published report has yet named.
  4. The critic independently re-tests every claimed gap with the deterministic engine and discards whatever it cannot reproduce. No agent marks its own work.
  5. The human gate hands the survivors to a named analyst as drafted candidate red flags. Accepting one records the analyst, the timestamp and their written reason; only then can the indicator enter the rulebook.

Whether a rule fired is decided by a deterministic rule engine, not by the language model. The model proposes scenarios; the engine decides what they hit. Generation is held at the level of regulatory indicators, so the system never produces a usable exploit.

Drift is a number you can move, not just describe: instituting six verified gaps as new indicators takes the Drift Index from 57.0 to 52.7 and coverage from 45.2% to 47.8%, measured in the app. All data in this prototype is public or synthetic — no personal data and no live systems.

Access and accountability

A gap report is an attack map, so the console is not open. Every page beyond this one requires a sign-in, and each account carries a role: an analyst can run checks and accept, amend, reject or institute findings; an observer can read the findings and the paper trail but cannot act on them.

Every action is written to an append-only trail with the person's name, organisation, role, session and timestamp, alongside the evidence, the rule defeated and the written rationale — so any promotion into guidance stays defensible on review.
